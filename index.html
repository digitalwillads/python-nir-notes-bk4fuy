Python near-infrared cameras never left the lab

A loose group of people, not a company, did this research. Python skin stays dark in near-infrared light, and live plants turn bright. The idea never left the lab. For the nest hunt, it may still help one witness.

Drag across the picture: the left side is what your eye sees, the right side is an 850 nm camera.

A loose group of people, not a company, did Florida's near-infrared (NIR) python detection research. Optics professor Ron Driggers led it from 2018 to 2021. He then held a UCF professorship and a part-time post at imec USA, a nonprofit research institute in Kissimmee. His partners were imec staff in Florida and Belgium, UCF students and UF python biologist Frank Mazzotti. The last partner was Extended Reality Systems, a one-man Florida LLC that the state dissolved in 2021. Embry-Riddle ran a separate drone study. The papers prove one thing well: python skin stays dark in NIR light, and live plants turn bright. They prove much less about detection. Observers who looked at still images found pythons at 1.20 to 1.34 times the range of a color camera. A drone test raised hits at 15 m from 0.59 to 0.94. Every study used one to three pythons, mostly dead, in a box, grass fields or on one levee. Since 2021, no product, patent or field program followed. imec's Florida center turned to superconducting electronics, and the team scattered. Florida programs still find pythons by eye, with scout snakes, dogs and lure cameras. For Killer Conservation's nest hunt, the news is mixed (judgment). No NIR camera sees a python coiled under cover. But live grass glows in NIR light and crushed or dying grass does not. So an NIR band could help the camera witness spot the disturbed patch at a nest opening. Nobody has tested that yet.

Warnings before you use these numbers

  • No field rate exists. No study measured NIR detection of live, free-ranging pythons in the Everglades. All numbers come from one to three pythons, mostly dead and thawed.
  • Most numbers measure people, not machines. Observers clicked on still images or short videos. The only published automatic NIR result comes from 15 test images of one dead python.
  • Press figures mix conditions. "20% farther", "1.3 times" and "35% better" each come from one narrow condition. The press table below shows each one.
  • Two range figures are extrapolations (judgment). Some 50% detection ranges lie beyond the farthest python that the team placed. They come from a fitted curve, not from a measurement.
  • NIR finds snakes, not species. Driggers said: "It allows us to detect snakes, not classify species of snakes" (The Wildlife Society 2019). In NIR, a python also looks like a shadow (Hewitt 2024).
  • The newest work is not verified. The researchers read only the library record of Li Zhang's 2026 dissertation, not its text.

Python skin stays dark where live plants turn bright above 750 nm

The 2019 reflectivity paper is the base of everything that follows. The team measured three pythons in a white box at UF's Fort Lauderdale center in November 2018. Python 1 was about 3 months old and 85 cm long. Python 2 was a young wild-caught snake. Python 3 was a dead, thawed snake of about 2 m. They used imec's SNAPSCAN hyperspectral camera, which records 150 bands from 470 to 900 nm. They compared the snakes with 20 plant samples, 10 alive and 10 dead (Driggers et al. 2019).

The result is clear. Live plants show "a dramatic increase in reflectivity just above 680 nm." Dead plants reflect 5% to 15% at 480 nm and 20% to about 60% at 900 nm. The python's light underbelly reflects about 0.3 to 0.4. Its dark pattern stays near 0.04 to 0.13 from 480 to 900 nm. The researchers read that value from a plot, not from the text. The authors conclude: "pythons could be discriminated from live plants very easily in the bands above 750 nm." Against dead plants the gap is "smaller." The dead python's spectrum was "very similar" to the two live ones. Later studies use this one comparison to justify dead test snakes (Driggers et al. 2019).

The papers state the contrast but not its cause. Plant reflectance rises sharply above about 700 nm, a well-known effect of leaf structure. None of these papers names that mechanism or explains why python skin stays dark (judgment). Licensed hunters also told the team that pythons usually lie on gravel road edges, with live and dead plants behind them. So every camera in this research was built for snakes in the open, not for snakes under cover.

The companion SPIE manuscript tested automatic classifiers. It used three cameras and a spectrometer. The field photos show one dead python near Kissimmee, at 1 to 20 m, by day and under halogen light at night (Vaca-Castano et al. 2019). The table gives the share of pixels that each method labeled correctly. The test sets were small.

Method Python pixels found Other pixels labeled correctly Test set
Random forest, all 150 hyperspectral bands 0.855 0.859 15 of 79 hyperspectral images
Random forest, 25 selected bands 0.815 to 0.826 0.843 to 0.849 Same
Random forest, visible multispectral camera (16 bands) 0.244 0.783 12 images
Random forest, NIR multispectral camera (25 bands) 0.609 0.800 15 images
Deep segmentation network, 3 NIR bands (695, 809 and 952 nm) 0.827 0.995 (0.5% false positives) 15 images

The deep network is the only published automatic NIR python detector with numbers. It found 82.7% of python pixels and mislabeled 0.5% of background pixels. But it saw one dead python, at short range, and fewer than 100 training images. The authors called for "actual hardware implementation of the camera" as future work. A spectrometer also showed "promising discriminability" in the short-wave infrared below 1,400 nm. Both 2019 papers proposed a second camera in that band for dead plants and soil. Nobody built it.

Observers saw pythons 20 to 34 percent farther, in narrow tests

The 2021 study asked a simple question. Do people see pythons farther away in an 850 nm NIR image than in a normal camera image? The team used two dead FWC pythons, each 4 to 5 ft long and about 5 lb. It put them in grass at 10 sites, 3 to 17 m from the cameras. They shot 100 images per camera, half by day and half at night with 850 nm LED arrays and halogen lights. Thirteen observers clicked on each python or on "No Python" (Hewitt et al. 2021). The hit rate is the share of python images in which observers found the python. The 50% range is the distance at which observers found it half the time.

Condition Monochrome visible Color visible NIR NIR against color
Hit rate, day 0.39 0.69 0.73 +0.04
Hit rate, night 0.51 0.51 0.77 +0.26
50% range, day, all images 16.1 m 23.7 m 28.4 m 1.20 times
50% range, day, glint images removed 3.2 m 12.2 m 16.3 m 1.34 times
50% range, night 6.5 m 9.4 m 11.6 m 1.23 times

The ratios in the last column are arithmetic from the paper's tables. The paper's own text says NIR reached 50% detection "1.2 times farther than RGB Vis at night and 1.3 times farther during the day." The abstract says "20% longer ranges." Most day images were shot in mid-afternoon facing west, and sun glint on the snakes helped the color camera. So NIR's clearest gain is at night. The daytime 50% ranges of 23.7 m and 28.4 m lie beyond the farthest python at 17 m. So they extend the fitted curve past the data (judgment). The paper left gravel, dirt and sand backgrounds "for later." Its data are "not publicly available."

The 2023 Embry-Riddle study is the only independent check. The team flew a DJI Mavic 2 Pro with a full-spectrum conversion and an 850 nm filter, straight down at 3 to 15 m. It photographed a 3 m dead python on a levee in marl prairie, stretched out and coiled. 101 people, a convenience sample of staff and students, reviewed 55 images (Cerreta et al. 2023). The paper also says "Two 3-meter pythons were used," which conflicts with its method text.

Up to 12 m, the two cameras stay within 0.05 of each other. Only at 15 m does NIR pull ahead. The paper also compares the ratio of hits to false alarms: 6.05 for NIR against 4.74 for color. The test gives t(10) = 1.77 and p = .048. It calls this "a 1.3x greater rate." Ten degrees of freedom imply about a dozen pooled values, not 101 people's scores (judgment). The paper does not report false alarm rates by camera.

Hewitt's 2024 dissertation adds two NIR studies, both in daylight with dead pythons (Hewitt 2024). In the first, an NIR camera rode on a car roof at 5 mph past two dead 8 ft pythons in a grass strip. Fourteen observers watched the videos. Detection ran from 0.81 to 0.96, depending on where the camera pointed. A steeper downward angle beat a longer view, against the team's own model. The author wrote that "long average range to target limits probability of detection more than increased time on target benefits it."

In the second, a lab-built drone carried an NIR camera with a 35 mm lens straight down over one dead 157 cm python. Eleven observers judged the frames.

Drone height 10 m 20 m 30 m 40 m 50 m
Detection probability 100% 99.3% 95.5% 82.7% 68.2%

The fitted 50% range is 60 m, past the highest flight, and the fitted limit for 80% detection is 42.7 m. Observers made false alarms on 10% of frames on average. Wind blurred many frames, so the team kept "the most pristine" ones, which flatters the result (judgment). The author calls the task "exceptionally difficult." She also reports what the team saw in the Everglades: "grass that exceeds the python's height blocks it from view."

Every test python was 3 m or shorter. Florida's record python measured 19 ft (NPR 2023). The USGS-led synthesis of 2023 still lists infrared as "still being explored as a tool to increase detections during road cruising" (Guzy et al. 2023).

No product followed, and Florida still finds pythons by eye, dog and lure

The published imec, UF and Extended Reality Systems line ends with the 2021 paper. Web searches found no patent, no SBIR award and no product for any team member or for Extended Reality Systems. Nobody ran direct searches of the patent office, SBIR.gov or USAspending, so this negative result is not verified. FWC's python coordinator said in 2021, "We are evaluating whether or not this technology will be effective in the field" (Optica 2021). No source reports the outcome of that evaluation. One live thread: Li Zhang's 2026 dissertation, which nobody here has read

Li Zhang finished a UCF CREOL PhD in spring 2026, with Kyle Renshaw as committee chair. The title is "Infrared Systems And Their Application On Target Detection And Geolocation." The library record says it built an NIR system with active lighting on a moving platform. It also built a custom machine-vision detector for Burmese pythons in "marshy South Florida environments." The record says the detector gives few false alarms. It names no sponsor (UCF STARS).

The notes do not know the rest. They lack the hit rate, the false alarm rate and the size of the image set. They do not say if the pythons were alive or dead, where the team tested, or if the platform was a car or a drone. They do not say if this work is the 2021 FWC contract, or if a journal paper or a field trial followed. His CREOL page lists a 2024 Sensors paper that compares NIR and long-wave thermal for target location in clutter. The researchers did not read it (CREOL). This dissertation is the closest prior art for any Killer Conservation NIR camera (judgment). Read it in full before you choose the camera witness.

The one automatic detection method with peer-reviewed results watches fixed spots, not landscapes. In a 2021 study, live-rabbit pens with infrared time-lapse cameras made 21 of 22 python detections. Cameras recorded 375 snakes, and visual surveys recorded 10 (McCampbell et al. 2024). A 2025 follow-up used an object-detection model as a filter. It flagged 94.5% of blank frames and cut review time six-fold. It matched human review for snakes over 66 cm but missed more small snakes (USGS summary). The researchers read only the summary, and the model's name is not known. The widely reported October 2026 UF "AI" study is different. It applies machine learning to capture records, not to images (Palm Beach Post via Yahoo). Thermal fares no better. In an October 2026 night test by a python hunter, a thermal camera "offered little help," and a dog found the snake (The Cool Down via Yahoo).

For the nest hunt, near-infrared cannot see the coil, but it may see the disturbed grass

Warning: no NIR study looked at a nest. Every study used snakes lying in the open. Hewitt's dissertation says it plainly: "grass that exceeds the python's height blocks it from view" (Hewitt 2024). A brooding female coils under cover, in dense plants or a burrow. So no camera, NIR or color, is likely to see her (judgment).

NIR can still help one of the three witnesses. The 2019 data show that live plants brighten much more above 680 nm than dead plants do. Dead plants reflect 20% to about 60% at 900 nm, and live plants jump far higher (Driggers et al. 2019). So in an 850 nm image, healthy grass looks bright, and crushed, cut or dying grass looks darker. A nest site often shows flattened plants or fresh bare soil at the opening (judgment). An NIR band could make that patch stand out, even when the coil stays hidden.

Witness What it answers What NIR changes (judgment)
Lidar Is the spot raised and dry, and did its shape change? Nothing
Heat camera Is something warm here, night after night? Nothing
Camera Is there fresh soil, crushed grass or a new opening? An NIR band makes crushed or dying grass stand out against live grass

The test is cheap. Embry-Riddle got NIR from a consumer drone with a full-spectrum conversion and an 850 nm filter (Cerreta et al. 2023). That camera can fly in the same dawn session as the lidar and the heat camera.

Caution: the ranges of live and dead plants overlap. Patches of naturally dead grass will also look dark. So the NIR camera will raise false flags, and only agreement with the other two witnesses makes it useful (judgment).

The first test (judgment):

  1. Fly an NIR-converted camera and a color camera over the nests of known scout females, with USGS as the partner.
  2. Count how often each camera shows the disturbed patch at the opening.
  3. Count the false flags in the same area, such as dead tufts and old diggings.

Four groups hold what this test needs (judgment). Renshaw's Knight Vision Lab at UCF holds the newest NIR python detector. Driggers and Furxhi know the camera and band design. USGS tracks the scout females whose nests give known answers. Mazzotti's UF group and FWC supply pythons and permits. imec USA no longer does imaging work, so it is not a partner.

Conclusion

The NIR python work did not fail on physics. It stopped for lack of a buyer (judgment). The contrast is real and repeatable, but the money was short-term, the lead institute changed its mission, and the one state contract left no public trace. Florida's spending since then went to methods that raise encounters: lures, scout snakes, timing models and robot rabbits.

For the nest hunt, NIR is a side test, not a main witness (judgment). It cannot see a hidden coil. Its best use is in the camera witness, to flag crushed grass at a nest opening. The lidar and heat witnesses must carry the work. The real gap is the first field number: nobody has measured how often any camera flags a real python nest. A test over known scout-female nests would hold the only such numbers in existence (judgment).
